How sign-in works across your space

Every service in a space shares one sign-in. None of them handle passwords, sessions, or Google; they read two headers and trust them. This page explains why that is safe and what happens on the way.

Updated 24 September 2026 · 6 min read

The space is the boundary

A space is one deployment of the platform on one host, named by its domain — say acme.ikigenba.com. The auth service lives at auth.acme.ikigenba.com and works out the space from its own Host: it drops the leading auth. label and uses what remains for the session cookie's domain, the Google callback, and which return URLs it will honor.

Because the space is derived per request rather than configured, the same auth binary runs unchanged on every host. There is nothing to keep in sync when you create a new space.

A browser signs in

The first time someone opens a service, the host's nginx asks auth whether the request is signed in. It is not, so the browser is sent to sign in:

  1. auth redirects to Google with a random state that also carries a PKCE verifier and the page to return to.
  2. Google authenticates the person and sends them back to /login/google/callback.
  3. auth exchanges the code, verifies the ID token, and checks the account belongs to your Workspace domain.
  4. It sets the ikigenba_session cookie for the whole space and returns the browser to where it started.

From then on, every service in the space sees the same session. Signing out anywhere signs out everywhere.

auth talks to Google only when someone is actually signing in. It starts, serves, and answers identity checks with Google unreachable.

Discovery is lazy on purpose

auth learns Google's endpoints from its OpenID configuration the first time a sign-in needs them, not at startup. A failed discovery is not remembered, so the next attempt simply tries again. The cost is one extra round trip on the first sign-in after a restart.

What a service receives

For every routed request, nginx makes a subrequest to auth's /check and acts on the status:

StatusMeaningnginx does
200Signed inCopies the identity headers onto the upstream request
401No valid credentialRedirects the browser to sign in
403Known, but refusedPasses the refusal through

On a 200, your service receives exactly two headers it can rely on:

X-User-Id: u_7Q2M4K9P
X-User-Email: ada@acme.dev

nginx strips any copies a client sends, so a service never has to wonder whether a header was forged. Treat the id as the stable key; emails can change.

Only trust these headers behind nginx.

A service listening on anything other than its socket in /run/ikigenba is reachable without the check. Don't expose it.

Scripts and agents

Anything that isn't a browser uses a personal access token. Create one from your profile; the secret starts with ikp_ and is shown exactly once. Press Ctrl C before you close the page — only its hash is stored.

curl -H "Authorization: Bearer $IKP_TOKEN" \
  https://auth.acme.ikigenba.com/me

A token acts as you, with your identity, against every service in the space. Give each script its own token so you can disable one without breaking the rest.

  • 30 days for anything you're trying out.
  • 90 days for a scheduled job you'll revisit each quarter.
  • No expiry only for something you'd notice breaking.

When sign-in fails

The most common failure is signing in with the wrong Google account — a personal @gmail.com rather than the Workspace one. auth refuses it and offers to try another account. If Google itself can't be reached, the sign-in page says so; services that already have a session keep working.

Still stuck? Check the host with opsctl, or read the rest of the docs.
